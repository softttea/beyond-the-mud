Beyond the Mud: Understanding Emotional Resilience in Adolescents Experiencing Family Adversity.

Research doesn't support the simplistic idea that divorce automatically causes psychological problems. Outcomes can depend on factors such as parental conflict, quality of relationships, stability, socioeconomic circumstances, and available support.

Imagine being told that two people you love no longer want to live together. Then imagine being expected to choose sides. Maybe nobody explicitly asks you to choose. Instead, you hear comments about the other parent. You become the person who knows too much. You learn when to stay quiet. You start carrying emotions that were never supposed to belong to you. And eventually, you may start wondering: "Am I actually allowed to feel the way I feel about all of this?"

Sometimes, this happens because your parents are still acting like children themselves. When the adults around you can't handle their own conflicts, you can easily start feeling as though everything that happened is somehow your fault, as if you could have said something differently, behaved differently, or done something to prevent everything from turning out this way. But is this always connected to divorce?

No.

Teenagers can struggle with unhealthy relationships with their parents even when their parents are still together. They can find themselves caught between their parents' conflicts, dealing with emotional pressure, neglect, manipulation, unrealistic expectations, or simply trying to understand why their relationship with their own parents feels so difficult.

So what do you do? That's the basic question. And, to be honest, I can't give you one perfect answer. I can't tell you, "Do this, and your relationship with your parents will suddenly turn into a fairytale." Life doesn't work that way. Sometimes you can communicate, set boundaries, ask for help, and do everything you can, and the situation still won't change immediately. Because sometimes, you cannot change the people around you. But that doesn't mean you are completely powerless.

Maybe the first step isn't figuring out how to fix your family. Maybe it's figuring out what is happening to you.

Family adversity & adolescence

Family adversity is not one single experience. The circumstances surrounding a separation, including the level and nature of parental conflict, how children interpret that conflict, parenting practices, and the support available to them, can matter considerably. Research on children exposed to interparental conflict has found associations between children's responses to conflict and adjustment difficulties, including internalizing and externalizing problems and self-esteem difficulties. Importantly, not every child exposed to parental conflict develops these problems.

The event itself is not always the whole story. The environment surrounding it matters, too. Even children from the same family will never have exactly the same story about what they have gone through.

Family adversity can take many forms. It may include parental separation or divorce, persistent conflict between parents, emotional unavailability, neglect, instability, psychological control, or situations in which a child begins carrying responsibilities that belong to adults. These experiences do not affect every young person in the same way.

A systematic review of childhood adversities and mental health found associations between several forms of family adversity, including parental divorce or separation and family dysfunction, and later mental-health difficulties. At the same time, this type of research describes associations, not a simple rule in which one family event automatically produces one psychological outcome. That distinction is important.

A difficult childhood does not determine a person's future. But understanding what can make these experiences difficult may help explain why some situations leave such a strong impression.

But what exactly happens when the home becomes difficult?

Adolescence is a period of significant development. Young people are becoming more independent, developing their identity, learning to regulate emotions, and beginning to define relationships outside their immediate family. At the same time, the family remains an important environment. When that environment becomes unpredictable, highly conflictual, emotionally unavailable, or overly demanding, a teenager may have to deal with two challenges at once: growing into their own person while trying to adapt to what is happening around them.

Family adversity can be more than one dramatic event. Sometimes it is a pattern. It might look like:

  • constantly anticipating a parent's mood;
  • feeling responsible for keeping the peace;
  • being exposed to conflict between parents;
  • feeling pressured to take sides;
  • being given information about adult problems that feels too heavy;
  • moving repeatedly between households;
  • experiencing very different expectations or forms of discipline;
  • feeling that your own emotions have to wait because someone else's emotions are more important.

Not every difficult family experience causes lasting harm. Some young people develop effective coping strategies and strong support networks despite significant adversity. This is one reason why resilience matters.

Resilience does not mean that something did not hurt. It means that a person can continue developing, adapting, and finding ways forward despite difficult circumstances. Resilience is not pretending that the mud isn't there. It is learning how to keep growing while acknowledging that it is.

Divorce & parental conflict

Is divorce itself the problem?

Not necessarily. Divorce can be a major change in a child's life. It can mean changes in where they live, how often they see each parent, routines, finances, schools, relationships, and their understanding of family. But divorce and parental conflict are not the same thing.

A child may experience a separation in which both parents continue to cooperate, communicate respectfully, and protect the child from adult conflict. Another child may experience constant arguments, accusations, pressure to take sides, or repeated exposure to one parent's negative opinions about the other. Those are very different environments.

Research has shown that children's exposure to parental conflict is associated with a range of adjustment difficulties. However, this should not be understood as a simple sequence in which conflict inevitably results in psychological harm. Children's interpretations of the conflict, their emotional and behavioral responses to it, and the broader family environment all play an important role.

This is why asking simply whether a child's parents divorced may say very little about the child's actual experience. More informative questions are:

  • Was the child protected from exposure to adult conflict?
  • Were they able to maintain meaningful relationships with both parents?
  • Did they feel responsible for managing a parent's emotions?
  • Were they placed in a position where they felt compelled to choose between parents?
  • Were they able to express their emotions and concerns without fearing a negative reaction from a parent?

At the same time, asking highly personal questions about family circumstances is not always necessary or appropriate. Even when phrased politely, such questions may feel intrusive, particularly when a person has not chosen to share these experiences. When signs of distress are evident, the focus should be on approaching the person with sensitivity and finding forms of support that fit their circumstances.

Loyalty conflicts

"Am I allowed to love both of my parents?"

One of the most difficult experiences for a child caught in parental conflict can be feeling that affection for one parent somehow threatens the relationship with the other. A teenager might think:

"If I enjoy being at Dad's house, will Mom be upset?"
"If I disagree with Mom, am I betraying her?"
"Do I have to believe what one parent tells me about the other?"
"Am I supposed to choose?" or "Do I even have an opportunity to choose?"

A child should not have to become the judge of their parents' relationship. They should be allowed to have their own experiences with each parent.

Your parents' relationship belongs to them. Your relationship with each parent belongs to you. You are allowed to judge, to choose and to feel.

This does not mean ignoring harmful behavior. If a parent is abusive, threatening, or otherwise unsafe, seeking protection and professional support is more important than maintaining a relationship simply because they are a parent. But a child should not have to inherit one parent's opinion of the other.

Emotional boundaries & parentification

When a child starts carrying an adult's emotions

There is a difference between helping at home and being made responsible for a parent's emotional or practical wellbeing. Psychologists use the term parentification to describe situations in which young people take on developmentally inappropriate parent-like responsibilities.

These responsibilities can be practical, for example taking excessive responsibility for household functioning, or emotional, such as becoming a parent's confidant, emotional supporter, or mediator.

A large mixed-methods systematic review of 95 studies found that parentification can have both positive and negative associations, but emotional parentification in particular was repeatedly associated with depressive symptoms and broader internalizing difficulties. The review also emphasized that social support and individual circumstances can influence outcomes.

This distinction matters. A teenager helping around the house is not automatically being parentified. A teenager listening to a parent talk about a difficult day is not automatically being parentified. The concern begins when the child is repeatedly placed in a role that belongs to an adult. For example:

"You're the only person who understands me."
"I need you to keep this secret from your father."
"You need to help me deal with your mother."
"I don't know what I'd do without you."

A child may accept this role because they love their parents. They may even feel proud that they are needed. But love does not make an adult responsibility developmentally appropriate. Children can care about their parents without becoming responsible for their parents.

Healthy communication does not mean telling a child everything. It means giving them the information they need, in a way they can understand, without making them responsible for what belongs to the adults.

Long-term effects

Can childhood experiences follow you into adulthood?

In almost all cases, yes. But a difficult childhood does not mean that someone is destined to experience depression, anxiety, unhealthy relationships, or other problems later in life. Research shows associations and increased risks, with outcomes influenced by many factors. Some young people experience distress and depressive symptoms, while others develop coping skills or show resilience. Social support and other protective factors can influence these outcomes.

Two people can experience similar family circumstances and grow in very different directions. One person might become highly sensitive to other people's moods. Another might become particularly independent. One might struggle to set boundaries. Another might become very protective of them. One might avoid conflict. Another might confront it immediately. None of these responses tells the whole story of the person.

The purpose of looking at long-term effects is not to give teenagers a frightening prediction about their future. It is to help them recognize patterns. If you notice yourself constantly monitoring other people's emotions, feeling guilty when someone is upset, avoiding conflict at any cost, or believing that you are responsible for keeping everyone happy, you can begin asking:

"Did I learn this somewhere?"
"Do I still need to live this way?"

That question creates space for change.

So what does this research tell us?

There is no single experience called "family adversity." There is no single psychological outcome, and there is no formula that can predict who someone will become.

What research does show is that difficult family environments can have an impact, particularly when young people experience persistent conflict, instability, inappropriate responsibility, or a lack of emotional support. At the same time, protective factors, including supportive relationships and individual coping resources, can shape how young people respond to adversity.

The most useful conclusion is not "This happened to you, therefore this is who you are." It is: "This happened to you. Understanding how it affected you may help you decide what you want to do next."

Research can tell us about patterns. But statistics cannot tell us exactly what it feels like to be the teenager sitting in the middle of a family argument. That is why Beyond the Mud also looks at lived experiences.
