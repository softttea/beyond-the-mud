Stories

Research tells us what patterns we can observe. Lived experience shows us what those patterns can feel like.

About these stories. They are not presented as scientific evidence, and they are not one person's real account. Each one is a composite created from recurring experiences described in public discussions. They are here because behind every statistic is a person trying to make sense of what happened to them.

Some of them may feel heavy. You can take a break at any time, and support is available here.

Case 1 · Caught in the middle

"When I was young, I loved my dad and looked forward to spending time with him. But my mom would often say negative things about him in front of me, and sometimes she would use my time with him as a consequence if I didn't behave exactly as she wanted. I was too young to understand the adult issues between them. I just knew that I felt caught in the middle.

Those experiences affected how I felt about my mom far more than they changed how I felt about my dad. Years later, she still wonders why we never became close, while my relationship with my dad remained strong.

Looking back as an adult, I think the hardest part was being made to feel that loving one parent somehow came at the expense of the other."

Composite experience, shared for educational purposes

What might be happening?

This is an example of what can happen when a child feels caught in a conflict of loyalty between parents. When one parent repeatedly criticizes the other, or makes the child's relationship with the other parent conditional on their behavior, the child may begin to feel that loving or enjoying time with one parent is somehow disloyal to the other.

The effects can extend beyond childhood. A child may remember not only what was said, but how they felt when they were placed in the middle. Over time, this can affect the relationship between the child and the parent who involved them in the conflict. The parent may later see the distance in the relationship without recognizing how earlier experiences contributed to it.

What can you do?

If you experienced something similar, an important first step is recognizing that the conflict between your parents was not your responsibility to solve.

  1. Separate their conflict from your relationships. You are allowed to have your own relationship with each parent. You do not have to adopt one parent's opinions, anger, or accusations toward the other. Their conflict belongs to them, as long as your safety and well-being are not being threatened.
  2. Don't make yourself the messenger. If your parents are still in conflict, try not to become responsible for carrying their feelings, accusations, or messages to one another. When possible, you are allowed to step away from a conflict that does not belong to you.
  3. Notice the guilt. You might think: "If I spend time with Dad, Mom will be hurt." Or: "If I disagree with Mom, I'm being disloyal." Try replacing these thoughts with: "Their feelings are theirs. My relationship with each parent is mine." You are not responsible for managing an adult's emotional reaction to your relationship with another person.
  4. Build relationships based on your own experiences. As you get older, you can begin to separate what you were told about someone from what you have personally experienced with them.

Ask yourself:

  • How does this person actually treat me?
  • Do I feel respected around them?
  • Can I be myself?
  • Do I feel safe expressing my feelings?
  • Is this relationship healthy for me today?
  • Can I do something?

Your relationship with a parent can be complicated. You do not have to force yourself to feel love, forgiveness, anger, or closeness simply because someone expects you to. You were never responsible for keeping your parents' relationship together. You were allowed to love both of them. You were allowed to have your own feelings. You were allowed to enjoy time with one parent without feeling guilty about the other.

And if these experiences still affect you years later, that does not mean you are stuck in the past. It may simply mean that something from your past still deserves to be understood.

Case 2 · Managing everyone's feelings

"Absorbing so much negativity as a child, with no control over it because you're just that, a child, and these are your parents. Constantly finding yourself in the middle, managing their emotions instead of being allowed to experience your own. It's an incredibly heavy burden for a child to carry."

Composite experience, shared for educational purposes

What might be happening?

When a child is repeatedly exposed to adult conflict, they may gradually become more focused on monitoring other people's emotions than understanding their own. They may learn to watch their parents' moods, stay quiet, avoid certain subjects, or change their behavior in an attempt to prevent another argument.

For a child, this can create an environment in which being safe feels connected to keeping other people calm. Instead of simply being allowed to experience their own emotions, they may feel responsible for maintaining peace. Over time, this can send a confusing message: "My feelings have to wait because someone else's feelings are more important."

A child can become highly aware of other people's emotions while having very little opportunity to understand their own. They may learn how to anticipate everyone else's reactions without learning how to recognize and communicate what they themselves need.

What can you do?

If you recognize yourself in this pattern, start by paying attention to your own emotions without immediately asking what everyone else might be feeling. You can ask yourself:

  • What am I feeling right now?
  • Is this actually my responsibility?
  • Do I feel loved?
  • Am I trying to prevent someone else's reaction?
  • What would I want if I did not have to keep everyone else happy?
  • Do I feel that I have to stay quiet to be accepted?

Your feelings deserve space. You do not have to become smaller, quieter, or less visible in order to deserve love.

Emotional resilience is not about learning to tolerate everything silently. It also involves learning that you have a place in the room, that your feelings matter, and that you are allowed to express yourself without constantly managing everyone else's emotions.

Case 3 · Knowing too much

"Their emotional instability and their inability to keep personal details about one another to themselves meant that I was exposed to things I never needed to know. I was their child, not someone who should have been involved in the private details of their relationship."

Composite experience, shared for educational purposes

What might be happening?

Parents sometimes believe that sharing their own experiences with their children will help create an honest and close relationship. However, healthy communication does not mean that children need to know everything happening in their parents' lives.

The core issue is not simply that parents have problems. It is whether the child is given information that they are not developmentally or emotionally prepared to carry. Knowing that your parents are struggling is not the same as being made responsible for knowing why they are struggling.

Children can and should receive age-appropriate explanations when family circumstances affect their lives. However, problems can arise when information becomes excessively personal, emotionally intense, or inappropriate for the child's developmental stage.

A child may listen because they love and trust their parent, without realizing that they are being placed in a role they never asked for. They may begin to feel responsible for understanding the situation, protecting one parent, keeping secrets, or forming an opinion about the other.

What can you do?

If you grew up in this situation, it can be difficult to understand where your responsibility ends and your parents' responsibility begins.

You do not have to adopt one parent's opinion of the other. You are allowed to say that you do not want to discuss certain things, when it is safe and possible to do so.

If you are still a child or teenager and do not feel able to set these boundaries yourself, look for a safe adult outside the conflict: a relative, teacher, school counselor, therapist, or another person you trust. You were never supposed to carry an adult problem by yourself.

If this happened to you years ago, the same principle still applies. You may not be able to change what happened, but you can begin recognizing which feelings and responsibilities were placed on you that were never yours to begin with.

Healthy communication is not about telling children everything. It is about giving them the information they need, in a way they can understand, without making them responsible for what belongs to the adults.

You can understand your parents without becoming responsible for them.

Case 4 · Two homes

"The biggest pain in it is having to pack your stuff and move every week. That's still annoying now, but when you're younger, you get attached to your parents, so having to constantly decide where you want to live is really difficult. And the completely different discipline and parenting styles were honestly a nightmare as a kid. You have to constantly readjust yourself depending on which parent you're with.

Now it's almost become a thing: if one parent gets on your nerves, you just go to the other."

Composite experience, shared for educational purposes

What might be happening?

For a child whose parents have divorced, living between two households can create a different kind of difficulty. There may be no single dramatic event that causes the distress. Instead, it can come from the accumulation of constant adjustments: packing a bag, changing houses, adapting to different rules, leaving one parent and going to the other.

When children are younger, this can be particularly difficult because they are emotionally attached to both parents. Being asked where they want to live may feel less like making a practical decision and more like choosing between two people they love.

From a parent's perspective, dividing time between two households may seem practical or fair. But what is practical for adults can feel very different to a child. The child is not simply moving between two addresses. They may be moving between two different emotional environments.

One home may have strict rules while the other is more relaxed. One parent may respond to mistakes with punishment while the other responds differently. The child has to remember what is expected of them in each place and quickly adjust their behavior. Over time, this can become exhausting.

The problem is not necessarily that parents have different personalities or parenting styles. Parents are allowed to be different. The difficulty comes when the child feels that they constantly have to "reset" themselves every time they move from one home to another.

Why can this happen?

After separation, parents are often trying to build separate lives while continuing to parent together. They may develop different routines, expectations, household rules, and approaches to discipline. Sometimes these differences are simply natural.

However, differences can become more difficult when they are connected to unresolved parental conflict. A parent may consciously or unconsciously try to make their home seem like the "better" home, become more permissive than the other parent, or become stricter in an attempt to maintain control.

The child can then become caught between two different systems rather than feeling that both parents are working toward the same goal: providing stability. The child may eventually learn to use one household as an escape from the other. While this might seem like a harmless advantage, it can also indicate that the two homes have become competing environments rather than parts of a stable family structure.

What can a teenager actually do?

Understanding the problem is important, but for a teenager living through it, understanding alone is not enough.

You may not be able to control whether your parents argue. You may not be able to control whether they divorce. You may not be able to stop one parent from saying hurtful things about the other. You may not be able to decide where you live or change the rules in two different households.

But there may still be things you can control:

  • How you understand what you are feeling.
  • Whether you believe that their conflict is your fault.
  • Whether you allow yourself to love both parents.
  • Whether you allow yourself and others to love you.
  • How you respond when someone tries to put you in the middle.
  • Who you choose to trust.
  • Where you go when you need support.
  • How you take care of yourself when things become overwhelming.
  • Eventually, what kind of person and what kind of life you want to build beyond the circumstances you were given.

You may not be able to control the environment you grew up in. But you can gradually learn how to navigate it without losing yourself inside it.

Why these stories matter

These experiences look different. One involves loyalty and feeling caught between parents. Another involves constantly monitoring other people's emotions. Another involves being exposed to adult information that a child was not prepared to carry. The fourth involves the repeated adjustment of moving between two households. And the author's own story shows the burden of misplaced responsibility.

They will not affect every child in the same way. But they share an important theme: the child can become responsible for things that should remain the responsibility of adults.

That is why looking at family adversity is not only about asking what happened to a child. It is also about asking what the child was expected to carry. And that leads to a more useful question: what is actually within your control?
